THE LIVE MARKETPLACE · RESPONSIVE WEB

OVERVIEW

The problem behind the screens.

Owners and sitters moved through different tasks, but both needed to understand what was available, what had changed, and what to do next. Testing both roles exposed more than 40 UX and product issues, from ambiguous search modes to booking states that disagreed across screens.

WHAT I CONTRIBUTED

  • Discovery and listing-management flows for owners and sitters
  • Messaging, booking, cancellation, and support states
  • Microcopy, visual hierarchy, empty states, and status language
  • Annotated findings, QA checks, and product analytics review
01

EXPLORE + DISCOVERY

Make the user’s goal visible before showing the inventory.

Explore put sitter and listing searches alongside overlapping save and filter controls. I separated the tasks and made the current search state visible, so people could understand what they were seeing and change their criteria.

OWNER LISTING JOURNEY

02

MESSAGING + BOOKING STATES

Design one source of truth for a changing transaction.

Problem: A card could say Booking Confirmed while the timeline said the sitter had declined. Stale buttons still offered booking actions.

My redesign: The status, explanation, and action now change together. A declined invitation removes invalid controls and points the owner to Find another sitter.

DESTRUCTIVE ACTIONS

Make opposite choices look and sound different.

The cancellation dialog used “Cancel” to keep a booking and “Cancel Booking” to end it. I changed the safe action to Keep booking, retained an explicit destructive label, and gave the two actions distinct visual weight.

03

DASHBOARD + LISTING MANAGEMENT

Show what users can do now—and what becomes available later.

Problem: The dashboard mixed applications, active sits, support, and messaging. Contact-sharing rules were unclear and some message actions appeared twice.

My proposal: I organized the dashboard around the next task, with one message entry point per intent, clearer status, and plain-language rules for when contact details become available.

COPY EVOLUTION

01 / BEFORE

No contact-sharing explanation

Users could not tell when the owner’s private contact details would become available.

02 / LATER PRODUCT STATE

“Owner private contact details are hidden until the backend shares confirmed-sit support fields.”

A rule appeared, but its wording described the implementation instead of what users needed to know.

03 / MY RECOMMENDATION

“Contact details will be shared after confirmation.”

Keep the confirmation rule; say it in plain language.

SUPPORTING EXAMPLE · SUPPORT CHAT

Complete the human handoff.

When a user agreed to connect with support, the system needed to acknowledge the request, explain what would transfer, and provide one clear next action.

04

ADDITIONAL INTERFACE IMPROVEMENTS

The same standards across smaller moments.

Beyond the three core journeys, I documented targeted changes to Community, sign-in, the Welcome Guide, and reviews. These examples show how clearer navigation, memory, print behavior, and completion feedback extend across the product.

OUTCOME

From findings to decisions the team could use.

Documented: I recorded more than 40 UX, content, and product issues across owner and sitter journeys, with screenshots and reproduction steps.

Designed: I created interface and copy proposals for discovery, booking-state consistency, dashboard actions, and support.

Prepared for release: I translated critical rules into QA checks for status, permissions, privacy, messaging, and recovery so future implementation could be tested against the intended experience.

A booking card, its timeline, and its available actions should tell the same story.
